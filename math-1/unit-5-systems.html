The one genuinely new idea: NC asks you to explain why elimination is legal, not just perform it. The answer has a geometric picture that makes the whole method obvious, and it is in L2.

L1What a system is

A system of linear equations is two or more equations that must hold simultaneously. A solution is an ordered pair making every equation true — not just one of them.

In Unit 4 you learned that a line is the set of all solutions to its equation. So the solutions common to two equations are the points common to two lines — which is exactly where they cross.

The simplest possible system. Two numbers add to 10 and differ by 4. What are they?
$x + y = 10$  and  $x - y = 4$
Add the two equations: the $y$ terms cancel, leaving $2x = 14$, so $x = 7$.
Substitute back: $7 + y = 10$, so $y = 3$.
Check both: $7 + 3 = 10$  and  $7 - 3 = 4$. Both true, so $(7, 3)$ is the solution.
Checking means checking both A value that satisfies one equation and not the other is not a solution. Every check in this unit has to run through every equation in the system.

L2Why elimination works

Elimination looks like a trick: add two equations together and a variable vanishes. NC asks you to justify it, and the justification rests on two facts.

Fact 1
Multiplying an equation by a nonzero constant does not change its solutions. Graphically it is the same line, just written differently.
Fact 2
Adding two equations produces a third equation whose graph passes through their intersection point.

Fact 2 is the interesting one. If a point satisfies both originals, then adding the left sides and adding the right sides keeps it true — so the new line must pass through that point too. Play with it:

Now the method makes sense. Elimination is not a trick for making letters disappear. It is a deliberate search for the combination that produces a vertical line ($x = $ something) or a horizontal one ($y = $ something) through the intersection — because reading a coordinate off those is trivial.

Which student wrote an equivalent system? The original is $x - 2y = 4$ and $3x + y = 9$. Four students each replaced one equation:
Brenda: $x - 2y = 4$,  $6x + 2y = 9$
Evan: $x - 2y = 4$,  $6x - 2y = 18$
Kevon: $-3x + 6y = 4$,  $3x + y = 9$
Brie: $\frac{1}{2}x - y = 2$,  $3x + y = 9$
Brie. Her first equation is exactly $\frac{1}{2}$ times $x - 2y = 4$, and multiplying an equation by a nonzero constant preserves its solutions.

Brenda doubled the left side of the second equation but not the right — $2(3x + y)$ is $6x + 2y = 18$, not 9.
Evan wrote $6x - 2y = 18$, which is not any multiple of either original equation.
Kevon multiplied the left side of the first by $-3$ but left the right side as 4; it should have become $-12$.

The pattern in every wrong answer: the constant was applied to one side only. Multiplying an equation means multiplying everything.

L3Three methods, and when each is easiest

NC is explicit that you are not required to use any particular method — you choose, and you justify the choice. Here is when each one is the least work.

Best when

Graphing

you want to see the situation, or an approximate answer is enough

How: graph both lines, read the crossing point.

Weakness: awkward when the solution is not at neat coordinates.

Best when

Substitution

one equation already has a variable alone, or is easy to isolate

How: solve one equation for one variable, put that expression into the other.

Weakness: creates fractions when no coefficient is 1.

Best when

Elimination

both equations are in the form $ax + by = c$

How: scale one or both equations so a variable cancels when you add.

Weakness: needs a scaling step when coefficients do not already match.

The same system solved three ways
Restaurant specials. On Thursday the restaurant took \$467 for 21 vegetarian and 40 chicken specials. On Friday it took \$484 for 28 vegetarian and 36 chicken. What does each special cost?
Let $v$ and $c$ be the two prices.
$21v + 40c = 467$  and  $28v + 36c = 484$

Neither coefficient is 1, so elimination beats substitution here. Multiply the first by 4 and the second by 3 to match the $v$ terms:
$84v + 160c = 1868$
$84v + 108c = 1452$
Subtract: $52c = 416$, so $c = 8$.
Then $21v + 320 = 467$, giving $21v = 147$ and $v = 7$.

Vegetarian \$7, chicken \$8. Check Friday: $28(7) + 36(8) = 196 + 288 = 484$. Correct.
Trading cards. José had 4 times as many cards as Philippe. After José gave away 50 and Philippe gave away 5, they had equal numbers. How many did each start with?
$j = 4p$  and  $j - 50 = p - 5$

The first equation already has $j$ alone, so substitution is the obvious choice:
$4p - 50 = p - 5$
$3p = 45$, so $p = 15$ and $j = 60$.

Check: $60 - 50 = 10$ and $15 - 5 = 10$. Equal, as required.
Math club. 60 candy bars and 110 drinks sell for \$265. 120 candy bars and 90 drinks sell for \$270. What does one candy bar cost?
$60b + 110d = 265$  and  $120b + 90d = 270$

Double the first equation so the bar terms match: $120b + 220d = 530$.
Subtract the second: $130d = 260$, so $d = 2$.
Then $60b + 220 = 265$, giving $60b = 45$ and $b = 0.75$.

Candy bars are \$0.75, drinks are \$2.00. Check: $120(0.75) + 90(2) = 90 + 180 = 270$. Correct.

Vanishing variables are information, not failure When both variables disappear you have not made a mistake — you have found the answer. A false leftover means the lines are parallel; a true one means they are the same line. The trap is writing "no solution" for both.
Solve $2x + 4y = 8$ and $x + 2y = 4$.
Multiply the second by 2: $2x + 4y = 8$ — identical to the first.
Subtracting gives $0 = 0$, which is true.
Infinitely many solutions. The two equations describe the same line, so every point on $x + 2y = 4$ works.
The second equation added no new information. It was the first one in disguise.

L5Writing a system from a context

Two unknowns need two independent facts. Finding both facts in the wording is usually the hard part.

1. Name both unknowns with units. "Let $d$ be the number of dimes and $q$ the number of quarters."
2. Find the two different facts. Usually one counts things and the other totals a value, a cost, or a weight.
3. Write one equation per fact.
4. Solve, then check against the story.
Alexis has only dimes and quarters. They are worth \$5.80 in total, and she has 40 coins. How many of each?
Counting fact: $d + q = 40$.
Value fact: $0.10d + 0.25q = 5.80$.

Substituting $d = 40 - q$:
$0.10(40 - q) + 0.25q = 5.80$
$4 - 0.10q + 0.25q = 5.80$
$0.15q = 1.80$, so $q = 12$ and $d = 28$.

Check: 28 dimes is \$2.80, 12 quarters is \$3.00, totalling \$5.80 across 40 coins. Correct.
Notice the two equations measure different things — one counts coins, the other adds up money. Two facts about the same quantity would not have been enough.
The most common modelling error Writing the same fact twice. "She has 40 coins" and "there are 40 coins in total" are one fact, not two, and a system built from them has infinitely many solutions. If your equations turn out to be multiples of each other, go back to the wording — you have missed the second fact.

L6Systems of inequalities

Unit 4 ended with a single inequality shading half the plane. A system of inequalities shades several, and the solution is the region where all of them overlap.

A fundraiser with three constraints — the dark region satisfies all of them
The situation. A club sells hats and jackets. The budget is \$1500, they want at least 250 items, and they must order at least as many hats as jackets. Hats cost \$5, jackets \$8.
Budget: $5h + 8j \le 1500$
Quantity: $h + j \ge 250$
Ratio: $h \ge j$
Hidden constraints: $h \ge 0$ and $j \ge 0$. You cannot order a negative number of hats, and forgetting to say so is a common omission.
Is 125 hats and 125 jackets a workable order?
Quantity: $125 + 125 = 250 \ge 250$. Fine.
Ratio: $125 \ge 125$. Fine.
Budget: $5(125) + 8(125) = 625 + 1000 = \$1625$, which is over the \$1500 budget.

So no — it fails one constraint, and failing one is enough. A point must satisfy every inequality to be in the solution region.
Try 167 hats and 83 jackets instead: 250 items, hats outnumber jackets, and the cost is \$1499.
A note on what comes next Finding the best point in a feasible region — cheapest, most profitable — is called linear programming. NC states explicitly that it is not expected in Math 1. Here you only need to describe the region and test whether a point is in it.

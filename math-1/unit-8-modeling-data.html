Modeling Data

Fitting functions to real measurements — and being honest about how much the fit is worth.

MapWhat this unit covers

Every unit so far handed you a clean function. Real data is messy, and this unit is about choosing a model for it, judging whether the model fits, and saying carefully what it does and does not license you to claim.

The habit this unit builds: a model is a claim, and every claim needs a caveat. How well does it fit? Over what range? And does it justify saying one thing caused another? Almost always the answer to the last one is no.

L1One variable: shape, center, spread

Given a single list of measurements, describe it in three parts. Answering all three is what a complete description means.

Shape
symmetric, skewed left, skewed right, or roughly uniform. Read it off a histogram.
Center
mean or median — a typical value
Spread
interquartile range or standard deviation — how tightly packed the values are

Shape decides which pair to quote.

Roughly symmetric with no outliers: use the mean and standard deviation.

Skewed, or containing outliers: use the median and IQR, because they are resistant to extreme values.

The same data as a histogram and a box plot
What a box plot hides A box plot shows five numbers and nothing else. Two very different distributions — one with a gap in the middle, one without — can produce identical box plots. When shape matters, look at a histogram.

L2What an outlier does to each statistic

An outlier is a value sitting far from the rest. It does not affect every statistic equally, and knowing which ones it moves is the whole point of this standard.

Drag the last value away from the group and watch each statistic react
30

Sensitive to outliers

Mean — every value contributes, so one extreme value drags it.

Standard deviation — measures distance from the mean, so an outlier inflates it sharply.

Range — defined by the extremes, so an outlier is the range.

Resistant to outliers

Median — only the middle position matters, not how far the ends reach.

IQR — built from the quartiles, ignoring the outer quarters entirely.

This is why skewed data gets described with the median and IQR.

Do not delete an outlier because it is inconvenient An outlier can be a recording error, or it can be the most interesting value in the set. Investigate before removing, and if you do remove one, say so. Quietly dropping data is how a model comes to look better than it is.

L3Scatter plots and the least squares line

With two quantitative variables per individual, plot one against the other and describe what you see: direction, form, strength, and any outliers — the same four words from the 8th grade recap.

When the form looks linear, technology fits a least squares regression line: the line making the total squared vertical distance to the points as small as possible.

Two data sets, one fitted line each

A least squares line can be fitted to any scatter plot, including one with no linear pattern at all. Technology never refuses. Deciding whether the line deserves to be there is your job, and that is what residuals are for.

L4Residuals: was a line the right choice?

A residual is what the model missed: $$\text{residual} = \text{actual} - \text{predicted}$$

Positive means the model under-predicted; negative means it over-predicted. Plot the residuals against the input and the pattern — or lack of one — tells you whether the line was appropriate.

Randomly scattered around zero: a linear model is appropriate. The leftovers are just noise.

Any visible pattern, such as a curve: a linear model is not appropriate. The data has structure the line failed to capture.

The same two data sets, now as residual plots
The residual plot is more honest than the scatter plot On the curved data, the fitted line looks passable at a glance — it rises through the middle of the cloud. The residual plot removes the overall trend and magnifies what is left, and the U-shape it exposes is unmistakable. Always look at the residuals before trusting a line.

When residuals reveal a curve, fit a different family instead. NC expects you to fit an exponential model with technology when the data calls for one — the same functions from Unit 2, now serving real measurements.

L5Using a linear model, carefully

Once a line fits, interpret its parts in context — the same job as Unit 2's parameters, now with data behind it.

Slope
"for each additional unit of $x$, $y$ changes by about $m$" — stated with units, and hedged with "about"
Intercept
the predicted $y$ when $x = 0$, which is sometimes meaningless in context. Saying so is a correct answer.

Interpolation: predicting inside the range of the data. Usually reasonable.

Extrapolation: predicting outside it. Increasingly unreliable the further you go, because nothing guarantees the pattern continues.

Slide the prediction point in and out of the data range
6
Assess the prediction. A model of a child's height against age, built from ages 2 to 12, gives $h = 6a + 30$ in inches. What does it predict at age 40, and should you believe it?
$h = 6(40) + 30 = 270$ inches, which is 22 feet 6 inches.

The arithmetic is right and the prediction is worthless. Age 40 is far outside the 2-to-12 range the model was built from, and people stop growing in their late teens — the linear pattern does not continue.
Assessing validity means checking the input against the data range and against what you know about the situation, not just checking the arithmetic.

L6Association is not causation

This is the last idea in the course and arguably the most useful outside it.

A strong association means two variables move together. It does not mean one causes the other. A line through data is a description of a pattern, not an explanation of it.

When two things are associated, at least four explanations are available, and the data alone cannot distinguish them:

$x$ causes $y$
the explanation people jump to
$y$ causes $x$
the arrow could point the other way
A third factor
something else drives both — a lurking variable
Coincidence
with enough variables, some will line up by chance
For each association, which explanation is most plausible?
What would settle it Only a well-designed experiment, where the researcher assigns the treatment at random, supports a causal claim. Observational data — measuring what already happened — can establish association and nothing stronger, no matter how tight the fit.

✓Quick review

Describe one variable
shape, center, and spread — all three
Which center
mean and standard deviation if symmetric; median and IQR if skewed or containing outliers
Outliers
move the mean, standard deviation, and range; leave the median and IQR largely alone
Residual
actual minus predicted; positive means the model under-predicted
Residual plot
scattered means the line is appropriate; any pattern means it is not
Slope in context
the change in $y$ per one-unit change in $x$, with units and an "about"
Extrapolation
predicting outside the data range; check it against the situation before believing it
Association
is not causation; only a randomised experiment supports a causal claim

If you remember one thing: the arithmetic being right does not make the conclusion right. Every model in this unit needs a sentence about how well it fits, over what range it applies, and what it does not prove.

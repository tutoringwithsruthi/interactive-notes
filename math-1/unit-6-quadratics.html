Quadratic Functions

A third function family — and a rewriting trick that makes its most important feature readable at a glance.

MapWhat this unit covers

You have met linear and exponential functions. Quadratics are the third family: the variable is squared, and the graph is a parabola rather than a line or a curve that only ever climbs.

What Math 1 deliberately leaves out

The NC standards are explicit about the boundary here, and knowing it saves a lot of wasted effort:

No quadratic formula. You solve by factoring or by taking square roots, nothing else.

No completing the square, and no vertex form. Math 1 uses exactly two forms: standard and factored.

Square roots of non-perfect-squares stay as radicals. $\sqrt{21}$ is a finished answer; you are not expected to decimalise it unless a context asks.

All three arrive in Math 2. If a problem here seems to need one of them, re-read it — it almost certainly factors.

L1What makes a function quadratic

A quadratic function can be written as $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$ with $a \ne 0$. The squared term is what makes it quadratic; if $a$ were 0 you would be back to a linear function.

Compare the three families you now know

How you spot one

Equation: highest power of the variable is 2.

Graph: a parabola — it turns around exactly once.

Table: first differences are not constant, but the differences of the differences are.

What the sign of $a$ does

$a > 0$: opens upward, and the vertex is a minimum.

$a < 0$: opens downward, and the vertex is a maximum.

Falling objects always have $a < 0$, which is why they have a highest point.

The second-difference test For $y = x^2$ at $x = 0,1,2,3,4$ the outputs are 0, 1, 4, 9, 16. First differences: 1, 3, 5, 7 — not constant, so not linear. Second differences: 2, 2, 2 — constant, which is the signature of a quadratic. Exponentials never do this; they have constant ratios instead.

L2Multiplying out, and factoring back

In Unit 3 you multiplied two linear functions and got a quadratic. That is the connection this unit runs in both directions.

Forwards (expanding): $(x + 3)(x - 5)$
Multiply every term by every term: $x^2 - 5x + 3x - 15$
Combine: $x^2 - 2x - 15$
Backwards (factoring) is the same statement read right to left, and it is the harder direction.
Build a quadratic from its factors, or read the factors off a quadratic

Factoring when $a = 1$

For $x^2 + bx + c$, look for two numbers that multiply to $c$ and add to $b$.

Factor $x^2 + 4x - 12$.
Need two numbers multiplying to $-12$ and adding to $4$.
Pairs for $-12$: $(1,-12), (-1,12), (2,-6), (-2,6), (3,-4), (-3,4)$. The pair adding to 4 is $-2$ and $6$.
So: $x^2 + 4x - 12 = (x - 2)(x + 6)$
Check by expanding: $x^2 + 6x - 2x - 12 = x^2 + 4x - 12$. Correct.

Always look for a common factor first

Factor $-4x^2 + 8x + 12$.
Every term is divisible by $-4$. Pull it out: $-4(x^2 - 2x - 3)$
Now factor the simple quadratic inside: two numbers multiplying to $-3$ and adding to $-2$ are $-3$ and $1$.
Result: $-4(x - 3)(x + 1)$
Why it is worth it: pulling out the $-4$ turned an awkward problem into an easy one. Check for a common factor before anything else.

L3The zero product property

Factoring only helps because of one fact, which you have known since primary school without naming it.

If a product equals zero, at least one of the factors must be zero. If $AB = 0$ then $A = 0$ or $B = 0$.

That is why $(x - 2)(x + 6) = 0$ splits into two small equations: $x - 2 = 0$ or $x + 6 = 0$, giving $x = 2$ or $x = -6$.

Zero is the only number this works for If $(x-2)(x+6) = 12$ you may not say $x - 2 = 12$ or $x + 6 = 12$. There are endless ways to multiply to 12. The property depends entirely on the product being zero, so a quadratic equation must be rearranged to equal zero before you factor.
Solve $6x^2 + 13x = 5$.
First, set it to zero: $6x^2 + 13x - 5 = 0$
Factor: $(3x - 1)(2x + 5) = 0$
Split: $3x - 1 = 0$  or  $2x + 5 = 0$
Solve each: $x = \dfrac{1}{3}$  or  $x = -\dfrac{5}{2}$
The mistake this example was chosen to catch From $(3x - 1)$ it is tempting to read off the zero as $1$, and from $(2x + 5)$ as $-5$. Both are wrong. You must solve each factor: $3x - 1 = 0$ gives $x = \frac{1}{3}$, not 1.
The shortcut "flip the sign of the constant" only works when the coefficient of $x$ is 1. NC calls this out specifically, so expect problems built to test it.

L4Two solving methods, and choosing between them

By square roots

When the equation has a squared quantity alone on one side and no separate $x$ term, undo the square directly — remembering that squaring loses a sign, so two answers come back.

Solve $x^2 = 49$:  $x = 7$ or $x = -7$. Both square to 49.
Solve $3x^2 + 9 = 72$:  $3x^2 = 63$, so $x^2 = 21$ and $x = \pm\sqrt{21}$.
Leave it there. 21 is not a perfect square, so $\pm\sqrt{21}$ is the answer. Only decimalise if a context needs it — then it is about $\pm 4.58$.
Which method fits? Try both on the same equation
Stephen and Brianna disagree. Solving $(x - 4)^2 - 25 = 0$, Stephen says use a square root. Brianna says factor. Who is right?
Both are. The equation yields to either method.

Stephen: $(x-4)^2 = 25$, so $x - 4 = \pm 5$, giving $x = 9$ or $x = -1$.
Brianna: it is a difference of squares, $(x-4)^2 - 5^2$, which factors as $(x - 4 - 5)(x - 4 + 5) = (x - 9)(x + 1) = 0$, giving the same two answers.

Expanding confirms they are the same equation: both are $x^2 - 8x - 9 = 0$.
Stephen's route is fewer steps here. Being able to defend the choice is the point — not picking the "official" method.

L5Zeros, factors, and solutions are one idea

Three words get used for the same thing, and NC wants you to see they are the same thing viewed from three angles.

You are looking atThe wordWhat it means
An expressionfactors$x^2 + 4x - 12$ has factors $(x-2)$ and $(x+6)$
An equationsolutions or roots$x^2 + 4x - 12 = 0$ has solutions $x = 2$ and $x = -6$
A functionzeros or x-intercepts$f(x) = x^2 + 4x - 12$ has zeros at 2 and $-6$; its graph crosses there

Factoring is worth doing because factored form displays the zeros. Standard form hides them; factored form hands them over. That is the entire reason the rewriting is taught.

How many zeros does $f(x) = (x + 5)^2$ have?
One. Both factors are the same, $(x+5)(x+5)$, so both give $x = -5$.
Because the two zeros coincide, the parabola touches the x-axis at $(-5, 0)$ and turns around without crossing. That point is also the vertex.
A quadratic can have two zeros, one repeated zero, or none at all. A parabola sitting entirely above the axis never crosses it, which is the "no real solutions" case.
Which has the largest x-intercept?  A: $f(x) = x^2 + 4x - 12$  ·  B: $g(x) = (x+2)(x-5)$  ·  C: $h(x) = (x-1)^2 - 4$
A factors to $(x+6)(x-2)$, so its zeros are $-6$ and $2$. Largest: 2.
B is already factored, zeros $-2$ and $5$. Largest: 5.
C by square roots: $(x-1)^2 = 4$, so $x - 1 = \pm 2$, zeros $3$ and $-1$. Largest: 3.

B has the largest x-intercept, at 5.
Notice each one wanted a different method. Reading the form before choosing is most of the skill.

L6Finding the vertex without new machinery

The vertex is the turning point — the maximum if the parabola opens down, the minimum if it opens up. Math 1 finds it using something you already have.

A parabola is symmetric. Its two zeros sit at equal distances either side of the turning point.

So the vertex's x-coordinate is the midpoint of the zeros: average them. Then substitute that value back into the function to get the y-coordinate.

Find the vertex of $f(x) = x^2 + 4x - 12$.
1. Factor: $(x + 6)(x - 2)$, so the zeros are $-6$ and $2$.
2. Average them: $\dfrac{-6 + 2}{2} = -2$. That is the x-coordinate.
3. Substitute: $f(-2) = 4 - 8 - 12 = -16$.
Vertex: $(-2, -16)$. Since $a = 1 > 0$ the parabola opens up, so this is a minimum.
Watch the axis of symmetry sit exactly between the zeros

L7Quadratics in context

Anything thrown, dropped, or launched follows a parabola, so projectile problems are where quadratics earn their keep. Every feature has a physical meaning.

A diver. $h(t) = -5t^2 + 10t + 15$ gives height in metres, $t$ seconds after leaving the springboard.
Common factor first: $-5(t^2 - 2t - 3) = -5(t - 3)(t + 1)$
Zeros: $t = 3$ and $t = -1$.
Reject one: $t = -1$ is before the dive started, so it is not part of the answer. The diver hits the water at 3 seconds.
Starting height: $h(0) = 15$, so the springboard is 15 m up.
Highest point: average the zeros, $\dfrac{3 + (-1)}{2} = 1$. Then $h(1) = -5 + 10 + 15 = 20$ m at 1 second.
Note: $t = -1$ was still useful. It was needed to locate the vertex, even though it is meaningless as an answer.
The diver's flight, with every feature labelled
A coconut. Its height is $-4x^2 + 8x + 12$, with $x$ in seconds. (a) Factor it. (b) What do the zeros mean? (c) How long is it in the air? (d) How high does it get?
(a) Common factor of $-4$:  $-4(x^2 - 2x - 3) = -4(x - 3)(x + 1)$
(b) Zeros at $x = 3$ and $x = -1$. Only $x = 3$ is meaningful — it is when the coconut reaches the ground. $x = -1$ is a second before it was thrown.
(c) 3 seconds.
(d) Vertex x-coordinate is $\dfrac{3 + (-1)}{2} = 1$. Height there: $-4 + 8 + 12 = 16$. 16 units, at 1 second.

The y-intercept is 12, so it was thrown from 12 units up — and it rose 4 more before falling.
Always ask which solutions the context allows Algebra hands back every mathematical solution. Contexts reject some of them — negative times, negative lengths, fractional people. Saying which you are discarding and why is part of a complete answer, and it is the same habit as restricting a domain back in Unit 2.
